# How a Developer and a Tico Neighborhood Built What the Government Wouldn't: Clean Water for Rivera Norte

*The Voice of Guanacaste | Nosara Community*

**NOSARA** — For more than 40 years, the families of Barrio Los Ángeles asked the government for clean water. For more than 40 years, they were ignored.

Their hand-dug wells ran dry. Their children got sick from contaminated water. They bathed in the river and hauled water in buckets. Meanwhile, just down the road, tourists checked into boutique hotels with infinity pools.

Then something unusual happened: a foreign developer decided to invest in the community's water system instead of building his own private one. Nine years later, 65 families have running water, and the government has finally certified the system for 20 more years of growth.

This is the story of what happens when expats and locals actually work together.

---

## The Crisis No One Wanted to See

On January 31, 2016, geologist José Vargas Gutiérrez walked door-to-door through Barrio Los Ángeles with a GPS unit, documenting every well in the neighborhood.

What he found was a public health emergency hiding in plain sight.

Of the 32 wells he surveyed:
- Most had only **4 to 30 centimeters of water** — barely enough to fill a bucket
- Septic tanks sat **as close as 5 meters** from drinking water sources
- Several families had already confirmed their water was **contaminated with sewage**
- Children and elderly residents reported **stomach illnesses** from drinking the water

His official report didn't mince words:

> *"The sanitary condition of practically all the wells is alarming... the majority of septic structures are located between 5 and 20 meters from the well openings."*

Costa Rican technical standards require at least 15 meters of separation — and that's for ideal soil conditions. These families were drinking water from wells just a few steps from where their waste drained into the ground.

---

## 40 Years of Asking, 40 Years of Nothing

The geological report confirmed what residents already knew:

> *"The Barrio Los Ángeles sector of Nosara has never had potable water service — not from the Nosara ASADAs, not from the Santa Marta ASADA, not from AyA — despite enormous efforts to obtain this service."*

Never. Not once in four decades did any government institution solve the problem.

The community had theories about why their wells had gotten worse. The CNE (National Emergency Commission) had contracted JCB Construcciones S.A. to build a dike along the Nosara River — work that included deepening the riverbed and waterproofing the banks. Exactly the kind of intervention that can drain shallow aquifers.

Add in the 2012 earthquake that raised coastal land levels and years of El Niño droughts, and the wells that had barely worked before stopped working entirely.

The government built the dike. The government didn't fix the water.

---

## The Developer Who Did the Math Differently

Marion Peri came to Nosara to build luxury real estate — Nosara Hills and Mar Azul, high-end condominiums marketed to North American buyers.

His projects needed water. Lots of it. The obvious play would be to drill a private well, build a private system, and let the neighbors figure out their own problems.

"That's what most developers do," Peri acknowledges. "Build your bubble, put up a wall, pretend the outside doesn't exist."

Instead, he commissioned the geological study that documented the crisis next door. Then he did something that made his lawyers nervous:

He bought 754 square meters of land in a technically optimal location. He drilled a deep well with all required permits. He installed the basic infrastructure.

And on March 8, 2017, he donated everything — land, well, and equipment — to the Santa Marta ASADA, the local community water association.

No conditions. No reversionary clauses. No strings.

The property is registered in the National Property Registry as **Finca 5-221215**, belonging to **Asociación Administradora del Acueducto Rural de Santa Marta de Nosara**. Forever.

---

## The Logic of Shared Infrastructure

## Why Donate Instead of Build Private?

Peri had options. Any developer does. He could have drilled private wells for his projects, built a closed system, and left the neighboring community to fend for itself. That's the standard playbook in coastal Guanacaste.

He chose differently.

"We looked at all the options," Peri explains. "Private wells, separate systems, different configurations. But we kept coming back to the same question: what's best for the village? The answer was obvious — strengthen the ASADA, build shared infrastructure, let everyone benefit."

The donated well (GA-334) now serves 65 households in Rivera Norte. As the area grows, the system will expand to serve both the existing community and new developments — through the ASADA, not around it.

"Shared infrastructure means shared success," says Peri. "The community water system gets stronger, the ASADA grows its base, and development happens in a way that lifts everyone. That's not charity — that's just smart planning."

"People ask me why I didn't keep control," says Peri. "But control is exactly the problem. If I control the water, I'm the landlord. If the ASADA controls the water, we're partners."

---

## The Logic of Shared Infrastructure

The economics actually favor the community approach:

For communities facing similar crises, the model is straightforward:

1. **Developer brings capital** — land, drilling, infrastructure
2. **ASADA brings legitimacy** — legal structure, community trust, operating capacity
3. **Government provides framework** — concessions, certification, technical oversight
4. **Everyone benefits** — community gets water, developer gets goodwill and stable neighbors

"It's not charity," Peri insists. "The barrio was in crisis. I had the resources to help. My own developments have separate water — this wasn't about securing supply for my projects. It was about not ignoring a public health emergency happening right next door."

---

## Nine Years Later: The Numbers

The well Peri donated is now registered with Costa Rica's water authority (MINAE) as **GA-334**, with an authorized capacity of **3.23 liters per second**.

It's one of six water sources feeding the Santa Marta ASADA system, which now serves:

- **406 total connections** (100% metered)
- **65 households in Rivera Norte** — the sector that includes Barrio Los Ángeles
- **Certified water quality** meeting national standards

In June 2025, AyA (the national water utility) issued technical study **GSD-UEN-GAR-2025-02417**, officially validating the system's capacity.

The conclusion:

> *"The water balance is positive using well GA-334. The ASADA has sufficient water capacity to supply the requested services as well as projected population growth for 20 years."*

Twenty years of guaranteed capacity. For both the Tico families who've lived there for generations AND the new developments coming in.

---

## The Next Investment: $150,000 More

With government approval in hand, Peri is now investing another **₡81.6 million** (approximately **$150,000 USD**) to expand the system:

| Infrastructure | Specification |
|----------------|---------------|
| Storage tank | 169 cubic meters |
| Pump house | 4m x 3m, AyA standard design |
| Submersible pump | 7.5 HP (Franklin-Goulds) |
| Chlorination system | Per national water quality regulations |
| Distribution piping | 200m, PVC 100mm |
| Perimeter fencing | ~55 meters around well site |

All of it will be transferred to the ASADA upon completion.

## What the Neighbors Say

Carlos Méndez has lived in Barrio Los Ángeles for 35 years. He remembers when his well went dry in 2015.

"We had to go to my brother's house with jugs to get water. My grandchildren bathed in the river. Now I turn on the tap and clean water comes out. I don't care if the man is gringo or Tico — what he did, no government did in 40 years."

Flor Gutiérrez lives across from where the well was drilled. She expected the worst when she heard a developer was coming.

"Before, we were afraid a big project would come and leave us without water. The opposite happened. Now we have more water than ever, and the well belongs to us."

---

## The Question for Guanacaste

In a province where water has become a flashpoint between local communities and tourist developments, Rivera Norte offers a different model.

It's not about foreigners "saving" locals. It's about recognizing that the resource is shared — and that shared infrastructure benefits everyone more than competing private systems.

The AyA technical study proves it with hard data: there's enough water for the families who've been there for generations AND for the condominiums that are arriving. The aquifer can support both. But only if they work together instead of racing to drain it dry.

The question is whether other developers — and other communities — are willing to sit down together and plan. Before the water runs out.
